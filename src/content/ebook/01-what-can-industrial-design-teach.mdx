{/* ===== INTRO (short, multi-column) ===== */}
<section class="intro">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p>Every physical object carries constraints that are visible the moment you hold it: the tolerance of a joint, the cost of a material, the way a hinge wears after a thousand uses. Digital products hide those constraints behind a screen, but they never disappear.</p>
    <p>This chapter opens the book with a claim that runs through everything after it: the questions a good industrial designer asks about production — what can we actually build, at what cost, maintained by whom — are the same questions a technology leader should ask about architecture, data and operations.</p>
    <p>What follows is not a metaphor stretched thin. It is a working method, illustrated with the facts, frameworks and cases in the rest of this chapter.</p>
  </div>
</section>

{/* ===== LONGFORM: headline + portrait + balanced columns ===== */}
<section class="longform">
  <div class="wrap">
    <h2>The tolerance you can't see is the one that breaks first</h2>
    <div class="grid">
      <div class="figcol">
        <figure>
          <div class="placeholder">[ 4:5 portrait<br/>cover or contain per metadata ]</div>
          <figcaption>A designer's early sketches encode constraints long before manufacturing does.</figcaption>
        </figure>
      </div>
      <div class="textcol">
        <p>In physical manufacturing, tolerance is a number stamped on a drawing: how far a part can deviate before the assembly fails. Everyone touching the product agrees on it before a single unit is built.</p>
        <p>Digital teams rarely name their tolerances explicitly. A response time of "usually under 300ms" is a tolerance. A "we'll clean that up later" is a tolerance nobody wrote down — which is exactly why it tends to fail first, at the worst possible moment, in production.</p>
        <p>Naming the tolerance is not extra process. It is the fastest way to know, before you build, whether the thing you are proposing can actually be produced and maintained at the scale you intend.</p>
        <p>The rest of this book returns to this idea from different angles: as a question of platform architecture, as a question of portfolio governance, and as a question of what a team owes the thing it ships.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

{/* ===== PULL QUOTE + ESSAY ===== */}
<section class="quote-essay">
  <div class="wrap">
    <blockquote class="pullquote">
      “A product that ignores its own tolerances doesn't fail gracefully. It fails exactly once, in public, at scale.”
      <cite>— J. A. Sosa</cite>
    </blockquote>
    <div class="essay">
      <p>This is not an argument for slowing down. Industrial designers work under real deadlines and real cost pressure — often tighter than a software team's. The difference is where the pressure surfaces.</p>
      <p>A missed tolerance on a production line stops the line immediately: everyone sees it, and the cost of ignoring it is visible the same day. A missed tolerance in software often ships. It sits quietly until traffic, scale or time exposes it, usually far from the team that made the original call.</p>
      <p>Treating architecture, data contracts and operational limits as tolerances — written down, owned, and checked before shipping — moves that cost back to where it's cheapest to pay: before launch, not after.</p>
      <p>The chapters in Part II take this further, mapping specific physical-design habits onto digital-product decisions one at a time.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

{/* ===== FRAMEWORK / PROCESS ===== */}
<section class="framework">
  <div class="wrap">
    <h2>A four-step way to name a tolerance</h2>
    <p class="dek">A short working method used throughout this book, first applied here to a single product decision.</p>
    <ol class="steps">
      <li><div class="ic">1</div><h3>Name the constraint</h3><p>State the real limit — cost, latency, headcount — in one sentence.</p></li>
      <li><div class="ic">2</div><h3>Write the number</h3><p>Attach a measurable value, even a rough one, not an adjective.</p></li>
      <li><div class="ic">3</div><h3>Assign the owner</h3><p>One person or team who is accountable when it's exceeded.</p></li>
      <li><div class="ic">4</div><h3>Check before ship</h3><p>Verify against the number, not against how the demo felt.</p></li>
    </ol>
  </div>
</section>
