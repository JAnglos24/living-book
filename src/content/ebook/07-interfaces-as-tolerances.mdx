{/* 2 — INTRO */}
<section class="intro">
  <div class="wrap cols">
    <p>When you work in industrial design, you soon discover that a user's interaction with a product begins before the object exists. It begins with knowing which materials you can source, which machine you will produce on, and which mold you can afford. Many of the sensations we later credit to design are decided there: how firm or soft a grip feels, where one part ends and another begins, what it costs to repeat the object ten thousand times.</p>
    <p>A toothbrush shows that whole journey, because it is an everyday contact between hand, material and body. Almost nobody thinks about its mold, and yet every manufacturing decision reaches the mouth of the person using it. I use it as an example of something anyone can check against the object in their own bathroom.</p>
    <p>The question I want to carry from there into the digital world is simple. First: what can we make? Then: how can someone use it? And finally, the one that weighs most once the product is in a person's hands: what are we demanding of them when they use it every day? Chapter 5 introduced digital materials and Chapter 6 their preservation over the life of a product;<sup><a id="r6" href="#ref-6">6</a></sup> this one examines how those decisions reach the person through the interface.</p>
  </div>
</section>

{/* 01 — MATERIAL AND PROCESS */}
<section class="longform" id="sec-01">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p class="eyebrow">Part II — From Physical to Digital · Chapter 07 · 01 Material and process</p>
    <h2>What a Toothbrush Allows</h2>
    <div class="grid">
      <figure class="figwrap">
        <div class="figure">
          <img src="/assets/chapter-07-toothbrush-anatomy.jpg" alt="Unbranded toothbrush with a rigid body, soft grip zones and bristles on a neutral surface" width="1600" height="2000" loading="lazy" />
        </div>
        <figcaption>Figure 7.1 — Rigid body, soft grip zone and bristles: three decisions the user feels as one.</figcaption>
      </figure>
      <div class="textcol">
        <p>Consider a toothbrush made of a rigid body and softer grip zones. That combination is not decided on the drawing alone. Multi-component injection, the mold and the compatibility between materials shape its geometry, its joints and its surface: where the soft part can end, what thickness it allows, how it bonds to the body and what texture remains visible. The machine bounds what is possible; the design team still makes decisions inside those bounds.</p>
        <p>It helps to separate that body from what happens with the filaments. The documentation from ZAHORANSKY, a manufacturer of brush-making machinery, distinguishes the injection of one- or multi-component parts from the placement of the filaments and their finishing, which includes trimming and rounding the tips.<sup><a id="r1" href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> They are different processes and each one delivers different decisions: the first defines the grip and the shape; the others define what touches gum and enamel. Knowing which one a decision belongs to keeps us from blaming the mold for what is the filament's, and the filament for what is the mold's.</p>
        <p>The same goes for hard, medium and soft bristles. The commercial categories are useful for talking about perception, but the real response depends on a combination of material, diameter, length and arrangement of the filaments. Two brushes that both say "medium" can feel different. I am not comparing specific brushes here; I use the categories only to show that a sensation is the consequence of several variables at once.</p>
        <p>To follow the argument it helps to fix three words with a simple meaning. They resemble one another, which is why they get confused.</p>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="datawrap" style="overflow-x:auto">
      <p class="tlabel">Table 7.1 — Three terms that are not synonyms</p>
      <table>
        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Term</th>
            <th>What it means in this chapter</th>
            <th>In the toothbrush</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr>
            <td><b>Constraint</b></td>
            <td>A condition that bounds a decision, such as the available process, the space of a room or the budget</td>
            <td>The mold you can afford and the materials that are compatible with each other</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><b>Technical tolerance</b></td>
            <td>Permitted variation from a specification, for example in a dimension or in the fit between parts</td>
            <td>The admissible variation in a wall's thickness or in the fit between the body and the soft zone</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><b>Acceptance threshold</b></td>
            <td>The limit of an admissible response, such as actuation effort, the intensity of an alert or the duration of a task</td>
            <td>How much firmness the user tolerates before it becomes bothersome</td>
          </tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
    </div>

    <div class="prose" style="margin-top:2.5rem">
      <p>This chapter borrows the idea of tolerance from manufacturing and extends it to interaction design. The availability of a material is a constraint; its properties and permitted variations help establish its operating margin. People also show differences and limits that must be studied. That extension is an editorial proposal of this chapter: they are not technical synonyms and I do not use them as if they were.</p>
      <p>Let us push the logic to the extreme. With the right machinery and materials it is possible to manufacture a toothbrush with metal bristles. Nothing in the process prevents it. And yet being able to make it does not show that the combination is appropriate for the intended contact: that a way to produce something exists does not answer whether it should exist. This is the point at which the possibility of production stops being enough to justify a design.</p>
      <p>The user feels the consequence of these decisions every time they pick up the object and use it. In an application something similar happens, even if its materials are less visible.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

    <div class="prose">
      <p>Take a need everyone recognizes: finding your bearings and getting somewhere. A navigation service such as Google Maps serves it in a car, on a phone and on a tablet, and the need is the same in all three cases. What changes is everything surrounding the person who has it. I do not promise that every current version of the service offers exactly the same functions on every device; I use the example to compare scenarios, not to describe a product.</p>
      <p>In a car, attention is shared with driving. Google's design guidance for driving (Android Auto / Android Automotive OS) establishes that driving-related tasks take priority over any other, that information must be readable at a glance — it proposes a maximum of two seconds to read a piece of content and return to the road — and that no operation should require taking both hands off the wheel.<sup><a id="r2" href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> These are limits of context, not of screen: the same route calls for fewer steps, shorter instructions and voice.</p>
      <p>On a phone, during a walk, the hand is occupied, daylight competes with the screen, battery matters and attention is divided with the surroundings: the street, other people, the next crossing. What the interface must achieve is that the next action is recognizable and can be handled with one hand.</p>
      <p>The tablet, by contrast, tends to appear when someone is preparing a trip. There is more surface and room for unhurried browsing: comparing alternatives, seeing more context. But it is wise not to assume that every tablet has the same sensors or serves the same purpose as another.</p>
    </div>

{/* 03 — SENSORY AND COGNITIVE LIMITS */}
<section class="longform" id="sec-03">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p class="eyebrow">Part II — From Physical to Digital · Chapter 07 · 03 Sensory and cognitive limits</p>
    <h2>What People Can Absorb</h2>
    <div class="prose">
      <p>When an interface demands more than the human senses can process, the person begins to make mistakes. This is not carelessness; it is biology. Visual acuity drops under poor lighting, auditory discrimination degrades in noisy environments, and working memory holds only a few chunks of volatile information at once.</p>
      <p>Industrial design learned this lesson through physical switchboards and aircraft cockpits: grouping related controls, providing distinct tactile profiles, and establishing unambiguous confirmation signals prevented catastrophic operator error. Digital interfaces inherit the exact same human receiver.</p>
      <p>In aesthetic and perceptual studies, the concept of MAYA ("Most Advanced, Yet Acceptable") studied by Paul Hekkert and colleagues demonstrates that human preference balances on an edge between novelty and typicality.<sup><a id="r3" href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> Push an interface too far into unfamiliar interaction metaphors, and users abandon it; keep it purely conventional, and it fails to leverage new technical capabilities. The tolerance band is narrow and measurable.</p>
      <p>Consider audio feedback. Consumer products such as Marshall headphones rely on a distinctive guitar-riff cue to confirm Bluetooth pairing.<sup><a id="r4" href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> The sound is brief, instantly distinguishable from speech or notification chimes, and unmistakable in its meaning. It respects the ear's tolerance for interruption while providing definitive confirmation.</p>
    </div>

{/* 04 — SPACE AND OPERATION */}
<section class="longform" id="sec-04">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p class="eyebrow">Part II — From Physical to Digital · Chapter 07 · 04 Space and operation</p>
    <h2>The Envelope of Movement</h2>
    <div class="prose">
      <p>An interface does not float in a vacuum. It lives in physical rooms, vehicle cabins, warehouse aisles, or hospital wards. Every physical object claims an envelope of movement — the spatial volume required not just to place the object, but to enter it, adjust it, clean it, and safely exit.</p>
      <p>Consider an aircraft passenger seat or high-density auditorium chair. The seat itself occupies a fixed rectangular footprint. But the operational envelope includes the recline angle, leg clearance, tray table deployment, and aisle egress.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="object-figure" style="margin:2.5rem 0">
      <div class="frame">
        <img src="/assets/chapter-07-seat-exploded-transparent.png" alt="Exploded view of a modular seat showing mechanical articulation and clearances" width="1800" height="1200" loading="lazy" />
      </div>
      <figcaption>Figure 7.5 — Exploded view of a modular seat: the mechanical tolerances between chassis, pivot hinges and cushioning dictate the user's envelope of movement.</figcaption>
    </div>

    <div class="prose">
      <p>When interior planners configure a room, they face an unavoidable trade-off between nominal capacity and operational tolerance. Cramming maximum seats into a hall (Layout A) reduces aisle clearance to the bare legal minimum, creating bottlenecks during ingress and making cleaning tedious. Providing generous margins (Layout B) lowers nominal capacity but preserves rapid circulation and dignity of movement.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

{/* 05 — PHYSICAL ERGONOMICS AND HEALTH */}
<section class="longform" id="sec-05">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p class="eyebrow">Part II — From Physical to Digital · Chapter 07 · 05 Physical ergonomics and health</p>
    <h2>The Cost of Cumulative Repetition</h2>
    <div class="grid">
      <figure class="figwrap">
        <div class="figure">
          <img src="/assets/chapter-07-scanning-grips.jpg" alt="Industrial handheld barcode scanner in pistol grip and top-down holster grip" width="1600" height="1800" loading="lazy" />
        </div>
        <figcaption>Figure 7.11 — Handheld barcode scanner: pistol grip vs. top-down grasp. The angle of actuation dictates cumulative strain across thousands of cycles.</figcaption>
      </figure>
      <div class="textcol">
        <p>In logistics facilities, retail fulfillment hubs, and hospital pharmacies, warehouse workers and operators scan hundreds of packages every hour. A handheld scanner weighing 450 grams might seem trivial during a ten-minute executive demo. But repeat that scanning motion 2,000 times across an eight-hour shift, and that weight becomes an ergonomic hazard.</p>
        <p>The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) notes that awkward postures, repetitive motions, and forceful exertions are primary risk factors for musculoskeletal disorders in warehousing.<sup><a id="r5" href="#ref-5">5</a></sup> When an interface forces excessive wrist flexion or thumb hyperextension to reach an "Enter" touch-target, the product is directly offloading engineering shortcuts onto the biological joints of the worker.</p>
        <p>Ergonomics is not an aesthetic preference; it is a structural tolerance. If our software flows require three confirmation taps instead of one automated trigger, we are multiplying mechanical joint cycles by thousands each working day.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

{/* 06 — BALANCING CONSTRAINTS */}
<section class="longform" id="sec-06">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p class="eyebrow">Part II — From Physical to Digital · Chapter 07 · 06 Balancing constraints</p>
    <h2>When System Limits Collide</h2>
    <div class="prose">
      <p>Product leaders rarely get the luxury of optimizing a single tolerance in isolation. Industrial engineering is the discipline of negotiating conflicting constraints: weight versus battery longevity, tactile feedback versus waterproof sealing, visual richness versus rendering latency.</p>
      <p>In software interfaces, the tension between screen real estate and cognitive clarity follows the same law. Packing an enterprise dashboard with sixty live data widgets satisfies an executive's desire for comprehensive visibility, but it overwhelms the operator's visual field, increasing error rates during critical incidents.</p>
      <p>Establishing deliberate tolerances means drawing boundary lines: what is the maximum number of actionable alerts allowed per hour? What is the maximum acceptable latency between physical barcode scan and server confirmation? When these thresholds are defined as hard contractual constraints rather than aspirational guidelines, product teams design defensively.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

{/* 07 — FAILURE MODES */}
<section class="longform" id="sec-07">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p class="eyebrow">Part II — From Physical to Digital · Chapter 07 · 07 Failure modes</p>
    <h2>How Interfaces Break Down Under Stress</h2>
    <div class="prose">
      <p>A bridge does not fail on a calm, sunny day under nominal traffic. It fails when heavy winds, thermal expansion, and peak resonant loads align. Similarly, digital interfaces do not fail during rehearsed sales demonstrations; they collapse when network latency spikes, sunlight washes out the display, and an exhausted user is rushing to meet a deadline.</p>
      <p>When an interface exceeds its tolerance band, it enters one of three distinct failure modes: silent error, user adaptation fatigue, or catastrophic system rejection. In silent error, the user believes an action succeeded when it actually failed silently in the background. In adaptation fatigue, the human compensates for poor ergonomics until chronic strain forces sick leave. In system rejection, operators actively bypass the application using unauthorized shadow tools.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="datawrap" style="overflow-x:auto">
      <p class="tlabel">Table 7.3 — Four operational failure modes</p>

{/* 08 — THE ECONOMIC COST */}
<section class="longform" id="sec-08">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p class="eyebrow">Part II — From Physical to Digital · Chapter 07 · 08 The economic cost</p>
    <h2>What Exceeding the Margin Costs the Business</h2>
    <div class="prose">
      <p>Bad interface design is often dismissed as a cosmetic defect or a matter of user preference. In high-volume operations, however, fractional ergonomic friction translates into massive corporate liability.</p>
      <p>Consider an enterprise distribution network with 250 warehouse picking operators. In a typical month, the team executes 600,000 package barcode scans (hypothetical model). If poor scanner weight balance and ambiguous on-screen confirmation generate a 0.02% error rate — equivalent to 120 misdirected packages per month — and each misrouted parcel costs an average of $42 USD to locate, restock and reship, that tiny interface imperfection extracts over $5,000 USD in monthly waste, or $60,000 USD annually.<sup><a id="r7" href="#ref-7">7</a></sup></p>
      <p>Add to that the unseen costs of worker turnover, musculoskeletal compensation claims, and productivity loss, and the true price of "tolerating bad tolerances" dwarfs the engineering investment required to fix it.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

{/* 09 — METHOD (FRAMEWORK) */}
<section class="framework" id="sec-09">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p class="eyebrow">Part II — From Physical to Digital · Chapter 07 · 09 Method</p>
    <h2>Measure Before Asking People to Adapt</h2>
    <p class="lede">Identify, measure, decide, validate: a four-stage method that repeats every time the hardware, the flow or the pace of work changes.</p>

    <div class="datawrap" style="margin-bottom:2.5rem;overflow-x:auto">
      <p class="tlabel">Table 7.4 — Six layers of the map of limits</p>
      <table>
        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Layer</th>
            <th>Example variable</th>
            <th>Evidence needed to decide</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr>
            <td><b>Material and process</b></td>
            <td>Variation in a part or grip response</td>
            <td>Specification and manufacturing tests</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><b>Device and context</b></td>
            <td>Actuation, reading and steps during the task</td>
            <td>Tests on the intended device and in the intended situation</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><b>Sensory and cognitive</b></td>
            <td>Alert frequency, errors and recovery</td>
            <td>Research with diverse users and representative exposure</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><b>Space and operation</b></td>
            <td>Spacing, movement and access</td>
            <td>Validation of the installed whole</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><b>Economics</b></td>
            <td>Effective use, cost and incremental value</td>
            <td>Scenarios and adoption tracking</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><b>Health</b></td>
            <td>Postures, force, repetition and duration</td>
            <td>Competent ergonomic evaluation and response to reported discomfort</td>
          </tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
    </div>

    <div class="steps">
      <div class="step">
        <div class="n">01</div>
        <h3>Identify</h3>
        <p>Recognize the exposure before measuring: which gestures repeat, for how long, in what posture and at what pace.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="step">
        <div class="n">02</div>
        <h3>Measure</h3>
        <p>Measure in representative conditions with diverse bodies and experience, looking at spread and not only average.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="step">
        <div class="n">03</div>
        <h3>Decide</h3>
        <p>Decide the adaptation based on verifiable requirements; never pass the adaptation on to the worker.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="step">
        <div class="n">04</div>
        <h3>Validate</h3>
        <p>Validate real sustained use week after week, evaluating again after changes in flow or pace.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

{/* 10 — RESPONSIBILITY */}
<section class="quote-essay" id="sec-10">
  <div class="wrap">
    <p class="eyebrow" style="margin-bottom:1rem">Part II — From Physical to Digital · Chapter 07 · 10 Responsibility</p>
    <h2 style="margin-bottom:2rem">What the User Ends Up Paying</h2>
    <div class="grid">
      <div>
        <blockquote>That person's health must have an explicit place in the requirements, in the tests and in our product decisions.</blockquote>
        <cite>&mdash; Chapter 07 &middot; 10</cite>
      </div>
      <div class="essay">
        <p>Back to the toothbrush. The metal bristles made an inadequate decision visible: nobody needs a study to see that they should not touch a gum. In a digital or hybrid product the effort is not so easy to see, because it is spread across alerts, gestures, grips, waits and corrections, each small and all repeated.</p>
        <p>The responsibility belongs to the product. It means bringing health and everyday exposure into device selection, into the definition of requirements and into the acceptance of the implementation. A successful demo needs to be complemented with evidence of representative use. And when tests reveal an avoidable load, what is called for is to revisit the object, the flow or the operation before passing on to the person the obligation to adapt.</p>
        <p>As product managers, we have to look at what happens after the first interaction. That device will stay in someone's hand throughout their working day, with the same gestures and the same demands we defined when we implemented it. Chapter 8 follows that trail: it is devoted to what changes when a product scales.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>
